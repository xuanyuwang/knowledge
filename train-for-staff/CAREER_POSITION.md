# Career Positioning Operating Brief

## Purpose

This file is the governing direction for career and resume work in `train-for-staff`.

Resume preparation is not primarily a writing exercise. It is a process of forming and testing a career hypothesis:

> What kind of senior software engineer am I, what value do I repeatedly create, and what kind of role should I target next?

The resume is a concise projection of that answer for a particular opportunity. It should not require redefining my career from scratch each time.

Use this loop:

> career hypothesis → resume and interview narrative → market feedback → refined career hypothesis

The goal is not to discover one permanent identity or maximize interview volume. It is to emphasize the most accurate and useful version of my professional identity for the next move, while improving the probability that the next role actually fits.

## Background

I have about seven years of software engineering experience:

- approximately three years in frontend engineering with JavaScript and React;
- approximately two years in backend and data work with Python, including data pipelines;
- approximately two years in backend engineering with Go, APIs, and distributed systems.

I have strong coding fundamentals and have practiced more than 300 medium-level LeetCode problems.

These are capabilities, but they should not be the primary definition of my value. Avoid positioning me mainly as someone who:

- knows Go, Python, or React;
- can solve coding exercises;
- implemented a list of features.

Instead, determine what the evidence shows about the problems I solve, the judgment I apply, the scope I own, the impact I create, and the environments in which I am likely to perform well.

## Core principle: evidence before identity

Treat this repository as evidence. Do not begin with a generic definition of Senior or Staff Engineer and force my experience into it.

For every material conclusion:

1. Inspect what I actually did.
2. Identify patterns across multiple pieces of work.
3. Infer the engineering characteristic demonstrated by those patterns.
4. Link the inference to concrete evidence.
5. Record missing or contradictory evidence.
6. Calibrate the strength of the claim.

Separate clearly:

- **fact:** directly supported by a source artifact;
- **interpretation:** a reasonable conclusion from several facts;
- **hypothesis:** a positioning statement that still needs testing;
- **unsupported claim:** too strong for the available evidence.

Never inflate scope, blur team outcomes into personal contribution, or invent impact.

## Workstream 1: Maintain the evidence inventory

For significant projects and incidents, recover the following where possible.

### Context and impact

- What system, product, customer, or workflow was involved?
- Why did the problem matter?
- What customer, product, revenue, reliability, operational, or engineering impact is supported?

### Problem and ambiguity

- What was known initially?
- What made the problem difficult?
- Was the initial problem statement incomplete or incorrect?
- What system boundaries, constraints, or failure modes mattered?

### Personal role

- What did I personally own?
- Which decisions did I make?
- What did collaborators own?
- Did I lead the framing, investigation, design, implementation, rollout, validation, or coordination?

### Judgment and execution

- Which hypotheses were tested or rejected?
- What tradeoffs were considered?
- How did the solution evolve?
- How did I decide what was worth solving and how much complexity was justified?
- Was an imperfect result accepted, and why was the residual risk reasonable?

### Collaboration and influence

- Which engineers, teams, PMs, support partners, customers, or leaders were involved?
- Did the work change a decision outside my own implementation?
- Did it enable or guide someone else's work?

### Outcome and validation

- What changed?
- How was the result validated?
- What measurable evidence exists?
- Did the work create reusable testing, debugging, migration, or verification capability?
- What remained unresolved?

Do not reduce projects to technology lists.

## Workstream 2: Test recurring strengths

Look for repeated evidence of characteristics such as:

- ambiguity resolution;
- end-to-end ownership;
- debugging depth;
- systems thinking;
- product and business judgment;
- reliability ownership;
- architectural tradeoffs;
- simplifying complexity;
- cross-team influence;
- proactive problem identification;
- execution under uncertainty;
- validation rigor;
- operational pragmatism;
- creation of reusable team leverage.

For every proposed strength, record:

1. the characteristic;
2. two or three supporting examples where available;
3. what each example specifically demonstrates;
4. counterevidence or limitations;
5. confidence: high, medium, or low.

One exceptional project may be a strong story without defining my overall engineering identity.

## Workstream 3: Calibrate seniority

Do not infer level primarily from years of experience or technical difficulty. Evaluate the evidence across these dimensions:

- **Problem scope:** defined task, feature, ambiguous system problem, cross-cutting problem, or organizational problem.
- **Ownership:** implementation, design, investigation, problem definition, coordination, and post-launch outcomes.
- **Decision quality:** pattern-following versus consequential technical or product judgment.
- **Influence:** own code, project, team, multiple teams, or broader engineering practice.
- **Time horizon:** immediate delivery, maintainability, migration, reliability, operational burden, and architectural evolution.
- **Ambiguity:** how much definition existed before the work began.
- **Business connection:** whether technical decisions can be connected to customers or company outcomes.
- **Leverage:** whether the work made other engineers or future work more effective.

Produce a nuanced assessment rather than a binary Senior-or-Staff label:

- where the evidence strongly demonstrates Senior behavior;
- where it demonstrates Staff-like behavior within a bounded scope;
- where broader Staff evidence remains weak, absent, or unmeasured.

## Workstream 4: Refine the career hypothesis

Maintain two to four genuinely different candidate descriptions when the evidence permits. A candidate might emphasize, for example:

- cross-system backend diagnosis and reliability;
- product-facing infrastructure and data correctness;
- domain stewardship and explicit business semantics;
- end-to-end ownership of ambiguous technical problems.

Do not select one merely because it sounds senior. For each candidate, assess:

- supporting and contradictory evidence;
- the work and roles it naturally targets;
- which parts of my background it deemphasizes;
- the risk of being too broad, too narrow, or difficult to prove;
- whether I actually want the day-to-day work it implies.

The current hypothesis may change. Treat interviews, recruiter conversations, job descriptions, and experience in new projects as feedback rather than as a one-time verdict on identity.

## Workstream 5: Define the next-role fit

The career model must answer both:

> Why should this company hire me?

and:

> Why should I take this job?

Derive target criteria from demonstrated strengths and stated preferences.

### Work to seek

Assess whether the role provides:

- meaningful ambiguity and problem-framing responsibility;
- backend, data, distributed-system, or product-infrastructure depth;
- ownership through production validation and follow-up;
- technically complex product domains where semantics matter;
- opportunities to create standards, tools, or reusable patterns;
- broader scope and influence without requiring unsupported claims about current level.

### Work to avoid or question

Screen carefully for:

- primarily well-scoped ticket execution;
- narrow ownership despite a senior title;
- repetitive maintenance without authority to address recurring causes;
- technology-specific work with little meaningful problem ownership;
- roles where ownership ends at code merge;
- a Staff title without a credible mandate, support, or transition path.

### Team and company fit

Evaluate:

- engineering maturity and operational discipline;
- autonomy and expected ownership;
- PM and engineering collaboration;
- team size and cross-team boundaries;
- manager expectations and growth support;
- pace, sustainability, and tolerance for quality work;
- whether startup or larger-company constraints fit the work I want.

Separate evidence-based preferences from hypotheses that still need real-world testing.

## Workstream 6: Derive resumes and interview narratives

Construct or tailor the resume only from a reasonably stable career hypothesis and verified evidence bank.

Every significant bullet should contribute evidence of one or more of:

- scope;
- complexity;
- ownership;
- judgment;
- impact;
- technical depth;
- collaboration;
- influence;
- leverage.

A useful shape is:

> problem or context → personal contribution and decision → relevant complexity → result

Do not mechanically force every bullet into the same grammar. Do not use a technology list as a substitute for value.

The resume should be a selective one-to-two-page projection for a role, not an exhaustive history. Interview stories can preserve more context, tradeoffs, failure, collaboration, and lessons.

## Quantification and claims rules

Look for useful evidence such as:

- latency or throughput;
- reliability or error reduction;
- customer and production scope;
- data or request volume;
- adoption and rollout percentages;
- engineering time saved;
- failure rates and experiment results;
- number of systems, APIs, or teams involved.

Never fabricate a metric. If a fact would materially strengthen a claim, record it as a recovery task:

> Missing evidence worth recovering: production frequency of this failure.

Distinguish:

- **technical significance:** how difficult, subtle, or sophisticated the work was;
- **organizational or business significance:** why solving it mattered.

A technically deep project can demonstrate capability even when business impact is limited. A technically simple change can demonstrate strong judgment when its product impact is large. The strongest stories usually support both dimensions.

## Missing-evidence discipline

Actively look for insufficient evidence in areas such as:

- PM and cross-functional collaboration;
- mentoring and delegation;
- technical or project leadership;
- architectural and roadmap influence;
- handling disagreement;
- adoption by other engineers or teams;
- reduction of engineering effort or operational burden;
- prevention of future incidents;
- business and customer impact;
- sustained ownership after launch.

Do not convert missing documentation into a negative conclusion. Use calibrated language:

> I do not yet have enough evidence in this repository to make this claim strongly.

Then identify the artifact, metric, story, or external feedback that would resolve the gap.

## Working style for future analysis

When performing career work in this repository:

- challenge vague or flattering claims;
- ask “so what?” when technical work lacks demonstrated impact;
- distinguish personal contribution from team outcome;
- surface Senior-level behavior that may have felt ordinary at the time;
- do not exaggerate ordinary implementation into leadership;
- do not undervalue difficult investigation or judgment because it did not launch a large feature;
- flag especially strong stories as **Strong career evidence** and explain why;
- identify technically impressive work that is nevertheless weak resume evidence;
- preserve uncertainty and update hypotheses as new evidence appears.

## Definition of success

The career model is working when it lets me answer clearly and with evidence:

1. What kind of engineer am I currently choosing to emphasize?
2. What value do I create repeatedly?
3. Which facts and stories prove it?
4. At what scope am I operating?
5. Where am I approaching the next level, and where is the evidence incomplete?
6. What work and team should I pursue or avoid?
7. What should a hiring manager remember after 30 seconds?
8. Which five to eight stories best demonstrate my value?
9. Which missing facts would most improve the model?
10. How should this model be projected into a resume for a particular role?

The resume is an important artifact. The more durable output is an evidence-backed, revisable model of my professional identity that improves resume tailoring, interviews, job selection, and future career decisions.
